Can I get unpaid commissions and bonuses after I leave a job in California?

Quit or fired in California with commissions or a bonus unpaid? See when they count as earned wages, what your final check must include, and what to save.

By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · November 9, 2026

The short version: Commissions you earned before you left are wages, and the Labor Commissioner's Office says they can't be forfeited once earned. Bonuses are paid with your final wages too, but whether you earned one depends on the promise your employer made. Many bonus plans can lawfully require you to still be employed on the payout date, so the exact wording matters.

You closed the deals. You hit the number. Then you quit or got let go, and the commission or bonus never showed up. Employers count on workers not knowing which of those payments they can still collect.

Are commissions and bonuses really "wages"?

Yes. The Labor Commissioner's Office says wages include all amounts for labor performed, whether set by time, task, piece, commission or another method (Lab. Code § 200). That means commissions you earned are owed on the same schedule as the rest of your pay.

Not everything labeled a commission counts as one. The Labor Commissioner's Office explains that commission wages come from selling a product or service, and the pay is a percentage of the price of what was sold (Lab. Code § 204.1; Keyes Motors, Inc. v. DLSE (1988) 197 Cal.App.3d 557). A payment for making a product or doing a service is usually something else, like a bonus or piece rate.

When is my last commission payment due?

When your job ends, the employer must pay all commission wages that are earned and can reasonably be calculated at that time (Lab. Code § 201, as explained by the Labor Commissioner's Office). If you quit without advance notice, those commissions are due within 72 hours (Lab. Code § 202). Our guide to final paycheck deadlines and waiting time penalties covers the timing in more detail.

When is a commission "earned"?

A commission is earned when you have perfected the right to be paid, meaning all the legal conditions have been met. The Labor Commissioner's enforcement manual adds that California courts won't enforce unlawful or unconscionable terms and will read ambiguities against the person who wrote the contract, usually the employer, to avoid a forfeiture.

Some plans are lawful, though. The Labor Commissioner's Office describes cases where:

  • A contract said no commission was owed on accounts paid more than a set number of days after the employee left, and the court upheld it (American Software, Inc. v. Ali (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 1386).
  • A contract was found overly harsh, the worker had no real choice in the terms, and commissions were treated as earned and payable after separation (Ellis v. McKinnon Broadcasting Co. (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 1796).

The result turns on the contract and the facts of the sale. If you were the one who quit, the manual says you generally can't recover on a sale that still needed substantial work you didn't finish, though that limit mostly applies to sales that take a long time to complete (Hudgins v. Neiman Marcus Group, Inc. (1995) 34 Cal.App.4th 1109). If you were fired, the manual says you may be able to recover all or a pro rata share, because the employer kept you from finishing.

Does my employer have to put the commission plan in writing?

According to the Labor Commissioner's manual, Labor Code section 2751 requires commission plans to be in writing and to state how commissions are computed and paid. The employer must give each employee a signed copy and get a signed receipt. If you never got one, that's worth noting. Save any emails, sales reports, and pay stubs that show how you were actually paid.

Can my employer change how commissions are calculated to cut my pay?

Not in ways that shift its own business costs onto you. The manual says deductions from sales that reflect the employer's cost of doing business can't be used when figuring "net" sales for a commission. The Labor Commissioner's Office also says draws against future commissions are lawful only if the draw is at least the minimum wage for all hours worked in each pay period.

What about bonuses?

A bonus is performance-based pay on top of your regular pay, and the Labor Commissioner's Office says bonuses must be paid under Labor Code sections 201 and 202 when employment ends.

Whether you've earned one is the real fight. The Labor Commissioner's Office says an employee who voluntarily quits before the payout date is generally not entitled to the bonus (Lucian v. All State Trucking (1981) 116 Cal.App.3d 972). But the manual also explains:

  • If the bonus promise was not expressly conditioned on staying employed, a worker who left may still be owed it once the other conditions were met.
  • A bonus plan generally can't be revoked after you start performing.
  • If you were fired without cause before the bonus period ended, you may be entitled to at least a pro rata share.
  • A bonus paid regularly in past years with objective criteria can become an implied contract. A truly discretionary bonus with no objective criteria and no routine payment doesn't.

Which documents matter most?

Your offer letter, the commission or bonus plan, emails about targets, and your pay stubs. Plans change quietly, so save every version you got. Screenshot sales dashboards before you lose access.

Does this affect my coworkers too?

Often. Commission and bonus plans are written once and applied to the whole sales team or crew. If a company wipes out commissions for everyone who leaves, or deducts business costs from everyone's sales totals, it may be a shared problem. Those cases can be brought as a wage class action. Unpaid commissions also change the numbers in your overtime rate, because the Labor Commissioner's manual says non-discretionary bonuses must be counted in the regular rate.

The takeaway

Before you leave, or right after, write down every sale and bonus target you hit, with dates and amounts. Ask in writing for the plan documents and for the commissions you've earned. If your final check leaves out money you earned, keep copies of all of it and get your case reviewed.

Is It Legal to Make Me Work Off the Clock in California?

No. California employers must pay for security checks, pre-shift meetings, after-hours texts and work done after clocking out. Here's what you can recover.

By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · October 23, 2026

The short version: No. In California, you must be paid for all the time you're under your employer's control or allowed to work, whether that's waiting in a bag-check line, sitting through a pre-shift meeting, or answering work texts at night. Your employer can't make you clock out and keep working, and the federal "it was only a few minutes" excuse doesn't cover routine off-the-clock work here.

Off-the-clock work rarely looks like theft. It's five minutes here and ten there, and over a year it's real money your employer keeps.

What counts as "hours worked" in California?

California's wage orders define hours worked as the time you're "subject to the control of an employer," including "all the time the employee is suffered or permitted to work, whether or not required to do so" (see, for example, IWC Wage Orders 4, 5 and 7, § 2).

That gives you two ways in. Time counts if your employer controls where you are and what you're doing, even when you aren't doing your actual job. It also counts if you're working and your employer knew or should have known about it, even if nobody asked you to.

Do I get paid for waiting through a security or bag check?

Yes. The California Supreme Court held that Apple had to pay retail workers for time spent waiting for and going through mandatory exit searches of their bags and personal devices, even when workers brought those items for their personal convenience (Frlekin v. Apple Inc. (2020)). The ruling applied to past pay, too.

What about getting ready before my shift?

The same control test applies before you clock in. Common examples:

  • Pre-shift meetings and huddles. If you're required to be there, it's work.
  • Booting up and logging in. Waiting for your computer or timekeeping app to load so you can start is time on the job.
  • Required gear. Putting on protective equipment or a uniform you must wear at the worksite can count as hours worked, depending on the job and the rules that apply.
  • Waiting to be let in. If you're told to be on site at 7:45 and the clock doesn't start until 8:00, those 15 minutes are the problem.

Can my boss tell me to clock out and keep working?

No. Clocking out doesn't change what you're doing. If you're still closing the register, cleaning up, finishing paperwork or helping a customer, you're working, whatever the time card says.

The same goes for working through lunch. If you eat at your desk while answering calls, or your manager pulls you back in halfway through, you may be owed pay for that time plus a meal break premium. Our guide to meal and rest breaks explains how that works.

Do after-hours texts, emails and calls count?

They can. If you're expected to answer your supervisor's messages at night, handle schedule changes on weekends, or take customer calls from home, that's work your employer is permitting and benefiting from.

A single quick message once in a while is a harder case. The Supreme Court left open whether time that's truly irregular and brief must always be paid. A regular pattern of after-hours work is much stronger, especially when your employer knows about it or expects it.

Isn't a few minutes too small to matter?

Federal law has a "de minimis" rule that lets employers skip paying for small, hard-to-track bits of time. California's wage statutes and wage orders don't adopt it. In Troester v. Starbucks Corp. (2018), a shift supervisor spent about 4 to 10 minutes a day after clocking out on closing tasks: sending the store's closing data, setting the alarm, locking up and walking coworkers to their cars. The California Supreme Court held that employers can't routinely require off-the-clock work of several minutes per shift without paying for it.

Ten unpaid minutes a day comes to more than 40 hours over a year of full-time work.

What can I recover for off-the-clock work?

You're owed pay for every hour you worked. Off-the-clock time also counts toward your daily and weekly totals, which can push you into overtime. Most hourly workers earn time and a half after 8 hours in a day or 40 in a week, and double time after 12 hours in a day (Lab. Code § 510). Fifteen unpaid minutes after an 8-hour shift should have been paid at the overtime rate. See how unpaid overtime is calculated.

Depending on how you're paid, unpaid hours can also mean you got less than minimum wage for that time. A worker paid less than the legal minimum wage or overtime can sue for the unpaid balance plus interest, reasonable attorney's fees and costs (Lab. Code § 1194). For minimum wage violations, you can also recover liquidated damages equal to the unpaid wages plus interest (Lab. Code § 1194.2). A court can reduce or deny those damages if the employer shows it acted in good faith and had reasonable grounds to think it was following the law.

How far back can I go?

Most unpaid wage claims must be filed within three years (Code Civ. Proc. § 338). Some can reach back four years under California's Unfair Competition Law (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17208). Every week you wait, another old week of unpaid time can fall outside the deadline.

Is this happening to my coworkers too?

Probably. Off-the-clock work usually comes from a company policy or a manager's habit. If everyone waits in the same security line, logs into the same slow system, or hears the same "clock out and finish up," everyone is losing the same time. That's the kind of problem a wage class action is built for, and a worker can also seek penalties on behalf of others under PAGA.

California law also bars retaliation for complaining about unpaid wages (Lab. Code § 98.6).

What to do right now

  • Write down your real hours. Each day, note when you actually started and stopped working.
  • Save the texts and emails. Screenshots of after-hours messages from your boss, with dates and times, show work your employer knew about.
  • Keep your pay stubs and compare them against your notes.
  • Note what you did off the clock: security line, meeting, closing tasks, calls from home.
  • Don't take company documents you aren't allowed to have. Your notes and the messages sent to you are enough to start.

If your notes and your pay stubs don't match, the difference is your money.

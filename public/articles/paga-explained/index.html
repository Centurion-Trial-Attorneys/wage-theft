What Is PAGA in California, and How Does It Work?

PAGA lets one California worker recover Labor Code penalties for the whole crew. Who can file, the LWDA notice, the one-year deadline and how penalties split.

By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · October 22, 2026

The short version: PAGA, California's Private Attorneys General Act, lets a worker who personally suffered a Labor Code violation sue for civil penalties on behalf of the state and every other employee hit by the same violation (Lab. Code § 2698 and following). You first file a written notice with the state's Labor and Workforce Development Agency, then generally wait 65 days before going to court. Most PAGA claims must start within one year, and workers receive 35% of the penalties.

The state doesn't have enough investigators to chase every employer that skips breaks or shorts overtime. So the Legislature deputized workers. With PAGA, you step into the state's shoes and go after penalties for the whole crew.

What is PAGA?

The Private Attorneys General Act (Lab. Code § 2698 and following) allows an "aggrieved employee" to bring a lawsuit for civil penalties for Labor Code violations. The claim is brought on behalf of the state, for violations against you and against other current or former employees (Lab. Code § 2699).

Where a Labor Code section doesn't set a penalty amount, PAGA supplies a default one (Lab. Code § 2699(f)). That lets PAGA reach common violations like missed breaks, unpaid overtime, off-the-clock work and inaccurate pay stubs.

Who can bring a PAGA claim?

You have to be an "aggrieved employee." Under the current law, that means someone employed by the company who personally suffered each of the violations alleged, within the one-year limitations period (Lab. Code § 2699(c)(1); Code Civ. Proc. § 340).

That "each" came from the 2024 PAGA reform, and it matters. If your manager skipped your rest breaks, you can go after rest break penalties for everyone who lost them. You can't add a claim about another department's commission plan that never touched your paycheck.

How does the notice to the state work?

You can't go straight to court. First you (usually through your lawyer) file a written notice online with the Labor and Workforce Development Agency (LWDA) and send it to your employer by certified mail. The notice has to identify the specific Labor Code sections violated and the facts and theories that support each one. The filing fee is $75, and it can be waived for workers who can't afford it (Lab. Code § 2699.3(a)(1)).

Then the clock runs:

  • The LWDA has 60 calendar days to say it won't investigate. If it sends nothing within 65 calendar days of the notice's postmark, you can file in court (Lab. Code § 2699.3).
  • If the LWDA decides to investigate, it gets up to 120 more days to do so and issue any citation.
  • Employers with fewer than 100 employees can submit a confidential proposal to cure the violations within 33 days of getting the notice (Lab. Code § 2699.3(c)(2)).
  • Larger employers can ask the court for an early evaluation conference after the lawsuit is filed, to try to fix the problem and settle early (Lab. Code § 2699.3(f)).

The time spent waiting on the LWDA doesn't count against your deadline to sue (Lab. Code § 2699.3(e)).

How long do I have?

PAGA penalties fall under California's one-year statute of limitations for penalty claims (Code Civ. Proc. § 340). Unpaid wage claims have a longer window, generally three years (Code Civ. Proc. § 338). Waiting shrinks a PAGA claim fast, because every month that passes drops older pay periods off the table.

How much are PAGA penalties?

When the violated section doesn't set a penalty, the default is $100 for each aggrieved employee per pay period (Lab. Code § 2699(f)(2)(A)). Multiply that across a large workforce and a year of pay periods, and the numbers get serious.

The 2024 reform added ways for that number to go up or down:

  • It can drop. If the employer took all reasonable steps to comply before it got the notice, penalties are capped at 15% of what's sought. If it took all reasonable steps within 60 days after the notice, the cap is 30% (Lab. Code § 2699(g)(1), (h)(1)).
  • It can double. The penalty rises to $200 per employee per pay period if a court or agency found the same policy unlawful within the past five years, or if the court finds the employer's conduct was malicious, fraudulent or oppressive. The reduced caps don't apply then (Lab. Code § 2699(f)(2)(B)).

Who gets the money?

The penalties are split. 65% goes to the LWDA for labor law enforcement and education, and 35% goes to the aggrieved employees (Lab. Code § 2699(m)). Before the 2024 reform the split was 75/25, so workers now keep a bigger share.

How is PAGA different from a class action?

They often target the same pay practices, but they work differently:

  • No class certification. A class action has to be certified by the court before it can proceed on behalf of a group. PAGA doesn't use that process, because you're suing on the state's behalf.
  • Penalties vs. pay. A class action recovers the wages workers are owed, like unpaid overtime and break premiums. PAGA recovers civil penalties.
  • Different deadlines. One year for PAGA penalties, generally three years for unpaid wages.

You don't have to choose. Many cases include both a class claim for back pay and a PAGA claim for penalties. If the problem came from a company-wide policy, read how wage class actions work.

What if I signed an arbitration agreement?

Arbitration agreements can split a PAGA claim in two. In Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana (2022), the U.S. Supreme Court held that an employer can enforce an agreement sending your "individual" PAGA claim (the violations you personally suffered) to arbitration.

The California Supreme Court then answered what happens to everyone else's claims. In Adolph v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2023), it held that an order compelling arbitration of your individual claims "does not strip the plaintiff of standing" to pursue the PAGA claims for other workers in court.

So an arbitration agreement may change where part of your case is heard. It generally doesn't wipe out the claim for the rest of the crew. Your agreement's wording matters, so have a lawyer read it.

Can my employer retaliate against me for bringing a PAGA claim?

California law bars employers from firing, retaliating or taking any adverse action against you for filing a complaint, starting a proceeding relating to your rights, or exercising your rights on behalf of yourself or others (Lab. Code § 98.6). Retaliation can be a separate claim on top of the PAGA case.

The bottom line

If you've been shorted on breaks, overtime or off-the-clock work, you probably weren't the only one. PAGA lets you hold the employer accountable for everyone, but the one-year clock starts with each violation. Write down what happened, keep your pay stubs, and talk to a lawyer before more pay periods slip away. If it's about breaks, start with California meal and rest break rules.

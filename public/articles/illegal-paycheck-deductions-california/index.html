Can my employer take money out of my paycheck in California?

Charged for a register shortage, a broken item, or your uniform? See which paycheck deductions California allows, which it bans, and how to get the money back.

By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · November 12, 2026

The short version: In California, your employer can take money out of your pay only when the law requires it (like taxes), when you agreed in writing to something like insurance premiums, or when a union or wage agreement allows benefit contributions (Lab. Code §§ 221, 224). Accidents, honest mistakes, and the normal costs of running a business aren't on you. If money came out that shouldn't have, you can get it back.

Your check is short again. Maybe the manager says the register came up $40 light, or you owe for the tray you dropped, or "uniform fee" shows up on your stub. California law puts tight limits on all of that, and many of these deductions are illegal.

What deductions are legal in California?

The list is short. According to the Labor Commissioner's Office, an employer can lawfully withhold money from your wages only when:

  • State or federal law requires or allows it, such as income taxes, Social Security, or a court-ordered wage garnishment.
  • You expressly authorized it in writing to cover insurance premiums, hospital or medical dues, or other deductions that don't amount to a rebate to the employer.
  • A collective bargaining or wage agreement expressly allows deductions for health, welfare, or pension contributions.

That framework comes from Labor Code section 224. Labor Code section 221 adds the basic rule: it's unlawful for an employer to collect or receive back any part of wages it already paid you. So an employer can't get around the limits by paying you in full and then demanding cash back.

Even your signature has limits. A written authorization covers things like insurance premiums and benefit dues. Signing a form doesn't override the separate rules below on charging you for shortages, breakage, or uniforms.

Can my boss make me pay for a cash shortage or something I broke?

Usually not. The Labor Commissioner's Office says that when a shortage or breakage happens by mistake or accident, the employer has to absorb the loss as a cost of doing business. Its examples include a waiter who drops a tray of dishes or a customer who walks out without paying.

There's one narrow exception. The Industrial Welfare Commission wage orders allow a deduction for a cash shortage, breakage, or lost equipment only when it was caused by a dishonest or willful act, or by your gross negligence (for example, Cal. Code Regs., tit. 8, § 11040, subd. 8). The California Supreme Court upheld the wage orders' power to regulate these deductions, describing a practice that made employees "in effect" insurers of the employer's merchandise (Kerr's Catering Service v. Department of Industrial Relations (1962) 57 Cal.2d 319).

The Labor Commissioner's Office is clear that an accusation isn't enough. It doesn't assume dishonesty or gross negligence just because the employer says so, and an employer that takes the money on its own does so at its own risk. If your employer thinks you stole, it can discipline you or go to court. It can't simply help itself to your paycheck.

Do I have to pay for my uniform or tools?

If your employer requires a uniform, it must provide and maintain it (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 8, § 11040, subd. 9(A)). The Labor Commissioner's Office says "uniform" includes clothing and accessories of a distinctive design or color, and the employer pays the cost.

The same goes for tools and equipment the job requires (subd. 9(B)). There's an exception: a worker earning at least twice the minimum wage can be required to supply hand tools that are customary in their trade or craft.

Your employer can ask for a reasonable deposit on items it gives you. If you don't return an item, it can deduct the cost from your last check only if you gave written permission beforehand, and it can never charge you for normal wear and tear (subd. 9(C)).

What other charges are off limits?

The Labor Commissioner's Office lists several:

  • Your tips. An employer can't take any part of a gratuity left for you (Lab. Code § 351).
  • Photos and bonds. If the employer requires a photo of you or a bond, it pays for it (Lab. Code § 401).
  • Medical exams. The cost of a pre-employment or other required medical exam can't come out of your pay (Lab. Code § 222.5).
  • Work expenses. Necessary costs of doing your job are the employer's to cover (Lab. Code § 2802).

Can my employer take a loan or advance out of my final paycheck?

Only in a limited way. The Labor Commissioner's Office says that if you agreed in writing to repay an employer loan in installments, the employer can deduct those installments. When you leave, it can take only one installment from your final paycheck, not the whole remaining balance, even if you agreed to that in writing (Barnhill v. Sanders (1981) 125 Cal.App.3d 1, as cited by the Labor Commissioner's Office). The employer has to collect the rest some other way.

An illegal deduction from your last check can also make the check short, and a willfully short final paycheck can trigger waiting time penalties. Our guide to final paycheck deadlines and waiting time penalties explains how those work.

How do I get the money back?

Start with your pay stubs. They should list each deduction, so you can spot a "shortage," "uniform," or "damage" line. Save them, along with any texts or emails where a manager told you that you'd be charged, and any form you were asked to sign.

The Labor Commissioner's Office says that if the loss wasn't caused by your dishonest, willful, or grossly negligent act, you're entitled to recover the wages withheld. You can file a wage claim with the Labor Commissioner or bring the claim in court. Its Deductions From Wages FAQ covers these rules in more detail.

Does this happen to my coworkers too?

Almost always. Shortage policies, uniform charges, and damage fees are usually written into a handbook or built into payroll for every cashier, server, driver, or warehouse worker. When the same deduction hits a whole crew, it may support a wage class action or a PAGA claim that covers everyone affected, not only you.

The takeaway

Look at every deduction on your pay stub and ask one question: is this a tax, a benefit I signed up for, or something the law requires? If not, write down the date, the amount, and what you were told, and keep copies. Mistakes and accidents are part of the employer's cost of doing business, and that money belongs in your paycheck.

Do Bonuses and Shift Differentials Raise My Overtime Rate in California?

In California, overtime is based on your regular rate of pay, not just your base wage. Here's how bonuses and shift differentials change what you're owed.

By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · November 5, 2026

The short version: Yes. California overtime is paid at 1.5 or 2 times your "regular rate of pay" (Lab. Code § 510), and that rate includes shift differentials, nondiscretionary bonuses, commissions and piece-rate pay, along with your hourly wage. If your employer figures overtime on your base wage alone, you're probably being underpaid on every overtime hour. Missed meal and rest break premiums are owed at the same regular rate.

Your pay stub might say $20 an hour. That doesn't mean your overtime rate is $30. If you also earn a night differential, an attendance bonus or production pay, your overtime rate should be higher, and payroll systems get this wrong all the time.

What is the "regular rate of pay"?

California law says overtime must be paid at no less than one and a half times "the regular rate of pay for an employee," and double time at twice that rate (Lab. Code § 510, subd. (a)). The statute doesn't say "base wage" or "hourly rate."

According to the Labor Commissioner, the regular rate is "the compensation you normally earn for the work you perform," and it can include hourly earnings, salary, piecework and commissions. For hourly workers, it includes "shift differentials and the per hour value of any non-hourly compensation the employee has earned." It can never be less than minimum wage.

For a quick overview of when overtime starts (after 8 hours a day, 40 a week, and on the seventh day), see our guide to unpaid overtime in California.

What pay counts toward the regular rate?

According to the Labor Commissioner, these generally count:

  • Shift differentials, like extra pay for nights, weekends or a hard-to-staff unit
  • Nondiscretionary bonuses, meaning bonuses for hours worked, for production or proficiency, or to keep you on the job, including flat-sum bonuses like attendance bonuses
  • Commissions and piece-rate earnings

A bonus is nondiscretionary when you were promised it ahead of time. If the handbook, offer letter or a posted program says you'll get $100 for perfect attendance, that's nondiscretionary. The employer doesn't get to call it a "gift" after the fact.

What doesn't count?

The Labor Commissioner lists some common exclusions:

  • Gifts for special occasions, like a holiday gift that isn't tied to hours or production
  • Discretionary bonuses the employer decides on entirely at its own option
  • Expense reimbursements
  • Pay for time you didn't work, like vacation, holidays or sick days
  • Certain weekend or holiday premiums, if they're at least 1.5 times your normal rate for the same work

How do you calculate the regular rate with two pay rates?

If you earn two or more rates in the same week, your regular rate is a weighted average. The Labor Commissioner's method: add up your straight-time earnings for every hour of the week, including overtime hours, and divide by the total hours you worked.

Say you work five 9-hour shifts (45 hours). You work 27 of those hours at $20 on days and 18 at $24 on nights with a differential.

  • Straight-time pay: (27 × $20) + (18 × $24) = $972
  • Regular rate: $972 ÷ 45 hours = $21.60
  • Overtime premium: half of $21.60, or $10.80, for each of your 5 overtime hours = $54

You're owed at least $1,026 that week. If payroll figures the overtime premium on the $20 base rate instead, you come up short every week you work nights and overtime.

How do bonuses change your overtime rate?

Bonuses get recalculated after the fact. The Labor Commissioner says overtime on a bonus must be paid in the pay period after the bonus-earning period ends. There are two main methods.

Flat-sum bonuses (attendance or weekend shift bonuses). In Alvarado v. Dart Container Corp. of California (2018) 4 Cal.5th 542, the employer paid a flat $15 for each day of weekend work. The California Supreme Court held that to find the bonus's per-hour value, the employer must divide it by the nonovertime hours actually worked in the pay period, not by all hours worked. The overtime hours are then paid at 1.5 times that value. The court said California's rule is "more protective of workers than federal law."

Here's the difference. You earn a $100 attendance bonus in a week you work 45 hours:

  • California method: $100 ÷ 40 nonovertime hours = $2.50 an hour. Overtime owed on the bonus: 1.5 × $2.50 × 5 hours = $18.75.
  • The method the court rejected: $100 ÷ 45 total hours = about $2.22. Half of that times 5 hours = about $5.56.

That's more than $13 in one week from a single bonus.

Production bonuses (pay tied to each extra unit or pick). According to the Labor Commissioner, these are divided by the total hours worked in the bonus period, and the overtime hours get an extra 0.5 times that amount (or 1 times for double-time hours).

Do meal and rest break premiums use the regular rate too?

Yes. When your employer doesn't provide a required meal or rest period, it owes you one additional hour of pay "at the employee's regular rate of compensation" for each workday it happens (Lab. Code § 226.7, subd. (c)).

In Ferra v. Loews Hollywood Hotel, LLC (2021) 11 Cal.5th 858, the California Supreme Court held that this means the same thing as the "regular rate of pay" used for overtime. It "encompasses all nondiscretionary payments, not just hourly wages." The court also refused to limit its ruling to future cases, so it applies to past pay periods too. Read more in our guide to meal and rest breaks.

Does this affect your coworkers too?

Almost always. Regular rate mistakes happen inside the payroll formula, so they repeat for every worker who earned the same differential or bonus, every pay period, until someone catches it. Each mistake can be small, but across hundreds of workers and three years it adds up fast.

That's why regular rate claims are often brought as a class action on behalf of everyone paid the same way, or as a PAGA claim. You don't need to recruit coworkers to start.

How far back can you go?

Claims for unpaid wages created by the Labor Code generally have a three-year deadline (Code Civ. Proc. § 338, subd. (a)). Every week you wait, your oldest week of underpaid overtime can fall out of reach.

What should you do now?

  • Pull your pay stubs for any week with overtime and a bonus, differential or commission.
  • Check the overtime rate. If it's exactly 1.5 times your base wage in a week you earned extra pay, that's a red flag.
  • Look for a bonus true-up. After a bonus period ends, there should be an overtime adjustment on a later check.
  • Save the bonus rules. Handbooks, offer letters and posted incentive programs show the bonus was promised.

If your overtime never seems to move when your bonuses do, the math may be wrong, and it's probably wrong for everyone on your crew.

Does my employer have to pay for my phone and mileage in California?

Using your own phone, car, or tools for work? California makes employers reimburse necessary work expenses. Here's what counts and how to get paid back.

By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · November 19, 2026

The short version: Yes, in most cases. California requires your employer to repay "all necessary expenditures or losses" you incur because of your job (Lab. Code § 2802(a)). That can include work miles on your own car and a phone the job makes you use. You can't sign that right away (Lab. Code § 2804), and unpaid amounts carry interest from the day you spent the money.

You drive your own car to job sites. Your manager texts you schedules and customer calls go to your personal cell. Maybe you bought your own tools or a printer for working from home. In California, those costs usually aren't yours to carry.

What does Labor Code section 2802 require?

Section 2802 is short and broad. It says an employer "shall indemnify" an employee "for all necessary expenditures or losses incurred by the employee in direct consequence of the discharge of his or her duties, or of his or her obedience to the directions of the employer" (Lab. Code § 2802(a)).

In plain English: if you spent your own money because the job required it, or because your boss told you to, your employer has to pay you back. The statute defines "necessary expenditures or losses" to include "all reasonable costs" (Lab. Code § 2802(c)).

Two words do most of the work. The cost has to be necessary, meaning reasonable for doing the job. And it has to come from the job, either your duties or your employer's directions. A personal expense you chose on your own isn't covered.

Does my employer have to pay for my cell phone?

If the job requires you to use your personal phone, that cost is a strong candidate for reimbursement under section 2802. Common examples:

  • Getting dispatch calls, schedules, or route changes on your own phone
  • Calling or texting customers for work
  • Using a required work app, clock-in app, or two-factor login on your phone
  • Taking photos or uploading paperwork the job asks for

How much your employer owes depends on how the phone was used and what it cost you. If your employer gives you a phone stipend, it still has to be enough to cover the reasonable cost of the work use.

Do I get paid for mileage when I drive my own car for work?

Yes, when the driving is part of the job: deliveries, trips between job sites, visiting clients, running errands for the business. The California Supreme Court has described several ways an employer can handle car expenses (Gattuso v. Harte-Hanks Shoppers, Inc. (Cal. 2007) No. S139555):

  • Actual expenses. The employer adds up the car costs you "actually and necessarily incurred" and pays that amount.
  • Mileage rate. The employer multiplies your work miles by a set rate per mile.
  • Lump sum. The employer pays a flat car allowance.

Many employers use the IRS business mileage rate. For 2026, that rate was 72.5 cents per mile through June 30 and rose to 76 cents per mile for miles driven on or after July 1, 2026 (IRS Announcement 2026-11). If your employer pays far less per mile than that, ask how it came up with its number.

A flat allowance isn't a free pass. The court said that when a lump sum turns out to be too small, "the employer must make up the difference." The same goes for employers that claim your mileage is built into a higher wage or commission. That works only if the employer has a way to identify which part of your pay is expense reimbursement, and only if that amount is "sufficient to fully reimburse" what you actually and necessarily spent (Gattuso). A vague promise that "the pay rate covers it" doesn't meet that test.

What other work expenses count?

Section 2802 isn't limited to phones and cars. Depending on the job, it can cover:

  • Tools, equipment, and supplies the job requires
  • Home internet, a printer, or office supplies you need because your employer has you work remotely
  • Travel costs like parking, tolls, and work trips
  • Required safety gear

Uniforms and tools also have their own rules in the wage orders. Our guide to illegal paycheck deductions covers when your employer has to provide and maintain them.

Can I sign away my right to reimbursement?

No. Any agreement "to waive the benefits" of this part of the Labor Code "is null and void" (Lab. Code § 2804). That's true whether the waiver is in your offer letter, a handbook, or an app's terms. A policy that says "employees cover their own phone and gas" doesn't change what the law requires.

What if my employer says I'm a contractor?

Gig and delivery workers often hear that expenses are their problem because they're "independent contractors." California presumes you're an employee unless the company passes the ABC test. If you've been misclassified, unpaid expenses can be part of what you're owed. See Am I misclassified as an independent contractor?

How do I get reimbursed?

Start by keeping records. A simple log does a lot of work:

  • Dates, start and end points, and miles for each work trip
  • Phone bills, plus screenshots of work calls, texts, and apps
  • Receipts for tools, supplies, parking, and tolls
  • Any written policy, stipend, or allowance your employer gave you

Then submit the expenses to your employer in writing and keep a copy. If it refuses or ignores you, you can bring a claim with the Labor Commissioner or file a lawsuit. Section 2802 adds two things that strengthen your claim: awards carry interest from the date you incurred the expense (Lab. Code § 2802(b)), and "necessary expenditures or losses" include attorney's fees you incur enforcing the law (Lab. Code § 2802(c)). Deadlines apply, so don't sit on it.

Does this affect my coworkers too?

Almost always. Reimbursement problems usually come from one company policy: a phone rule for every technician, a car allowance for every driver, a "use your own device" rule for every remote worker. When the same policy shorts a whole team, it can support a wage class action or a PAGA claim that covers everyone affected.

The takeaway

If your job runs on your phone, your car, or your own gear, your employer is supposed to pay for that use. Start a mileage and expense log today, save your bills and receipts, and ask for reimbursement in writing. If the answer is no or a lowball number, get your records reviewed.

**The short version:** Probably, unless your job passes two tests. In California, an employer can skip overtime for an executive, administrative, or professional employee only if that person spends more than half of their work time on real exempt duties, regularly uses discretion and independent judgment, *and* earns at least twice the state minimum wage for full-time work (Lab. Code § 515(a), (e)). In 2026, that's $70,304 a year. On January 1, 2027, it rises to $72,384.
</div>

Getting a salary feels like a promotion. You get a steady check, maybe a title with "manager" or "coordinator" in it. Then the 50-hour weeks start, and the overtime never shows up on your pay stub.

Plenty of California employers treat "salaried" and "exempt" as the same word. They aren't. Salary is just a way of paying you. Exempt is a legal status your job has to earn, and most jobs don't.

## What does "exempt" mean in California?

California overtime rules (Lab. Code § 510) cover most workers. State law lets the wage orders exempt three main groups from overtime: executive, administrative, and professional employees. Each exemption only applies if the employee (Lab. Code § 515(a)):

- is **primarily engaged** in duties that meet the test of the exemption,
- **customarily and regularly exercises discretion and independent judgment** in those duties, and
- earns a **monthly salary of at least two times the state minimum wage** for full-time work.

"Primarily" means more than half of your work time (Lab. Code § 515(e)). Your employer has to meet every part. Missing one means you're owed overtime like any other non-exempt worker, unless a different exemption covers your job.

The details come from California's wage orders. The examples below quote [IWC Wage Order 4](https://www.dir.ca.gov/IWC/IWCArticle04.pdf), which covers professional, technical, clerical, and similar jobs.

## How much do I have to earn to be exempt?

The salary floor is tied to the state minimum wage, with full-time work defined as 40 hours a week (Wage Order 4, § 1(A)). According to the Department of Industrial Relations:

- **2026:** at least **$70,304 a year**, based on the $16.90 state minimum wage.
- **2027:** at least **$72,384 a year** starting January 1, 2027, when the state minimum wage goes to $17.40 ([DIR announcement](https://dir.ca.gov/DIRNews/2026/2026-66.html)).

If you're paid less than that, you can't be exempt as an executive, administrator, or professional, no matter what your job description says. Check your salary against the new number in January. An employer that doesn't raise exempt salaries on time may have turned an exempt job into an overtime-eligible one.

Earning more than the floor doesn't settle anything. Pay is only half the test. Your actual duties are the other half.

## What duties make someone an exempt executive?

Under Wage Order 4, an exempt executive must (§ 1(A)(1)):

- manage the business or a "customarily recognized department or subdivision" of it,
- customarily and regularly direct the work of **two or more** employees,
- have authority to hire or fire, or have hiring and firing recommendations that get "particular weight,"
- customarily and regularly exercise discretion and independent judgment, and
- spend more than half of their time on that kind of work.

The wage order tells employers to look first at "the work actually performed by the employee during the course of the workweek" (§ 1(A)(1)(e)). So an assistant manager who spends most of the shift running a register, stocking shelves, or covering for no-shows is likely not exempt, even with "manager" on the badge.

## What about the administrative exemption?

This one gets stretched the most. An exempt administrative employee must do office or non-manual work "directly related to management policies or general business operations" of the employer or its customers (§ 1(A)(2)(a)). They must also customarily and regularly use discretion and independent judgment, and spend more than half their time on exempt work.

They also have to fit one of three roles: directly assisting an owner or exempt executive, doing specialized or technical work under only general supervision, or carrying out special assignments under only general supervision (§ 1(A)(2)(c)-(e)).

Following a script, entering data, scheduling, answering customer calls, or processing forms by the rules usually isn't exercising independent judgment. If your manager checks or approves most of your decisions, that's worth noticing.

## Who counts as an exempt professional?

The professional exemption covers people licensed or certified by California and primarily engaged in law, medicine, dentistry, optometry, architecture, engineering, teaching, or accounting (§ 1(A)(3)(a)). It also covers certain "learned or artistic" professions that require advanced knowledge or original, creative work (§ 1(A)(3)(b)). Professionals also have to use discretion and independent judgment and meet the salary floor (§ 1(A)(3)(c)-(d)).

Two exceptions surprise people. Registered nurses and pharmacists are **not** exempt professionals under Wage Order 4 unless they separately meet the executive or administrative test (§ 1(A)(3)(f)). If you're a salaried RN without overtime pay, look closely.

Computer software workers have their own exemption with a much higher pay floor: at least $58.85 an hour in 2026 (§ 1(A)(3)(h)), rising to $61.03 an hour or $127,108.34 a year on January 1, 2027 ([DIR](https://dir.ca.gov/oprl/ComputerSoftware.htm)). The wage order says "a job title shall not be determinative" for that exemption.

## What are the warning signs I'm misclassified?

- You're paid a salary but spend most of your day doing the same work as hourly staff.
- You "supervise" but can't hire, fire, or meaningfully weigh in on either.
- Your decisions follow a manual, a script, or a manager's sign-off.
- You're an RN or pharmacist with no management role and no overtime.
- Your employer changed your title, not your duties, when it moved you to salary.

## Does this affect my coworkers too?

Usually. Employers usually decide exempt status by job title, so if your position is misclassified, everyone with that title probably is too. Whole groups of "assistant managers" or "coordinators" can be owed years of overtime from the same decision. That's the kind of claim that can be brought as a [class action](/class-actions/) for every affected worker, or as a [PAGA claim](/articles/paga-explained/).

## What can I recover if I was misclassified?

A misclassified worker can sue for the unpaid overtime, plus interest, reasonable attorney's fees, and costs (Lab. Code § 1194(a)). That right applies "notwithstanding any agreement to work for a lesser wage," so signing an offer letter that called you exempt doesn't waive it.

Misclassification often brings other claims with it, like missed [meal and rest breaks](/articles/meal-and-rest-breaks-california/), since exempt workers rarely get them. Deadlines apply and older weeks age out, so see our guide to [unpaid overtime in California](/articles/unpaid-overtime-california/) for how far back you can go.

## What should I do now?

Write down what you actually do in a typical week, and roughly what share of your time goes to each task. Keep your offer letter, job description, pay stubs, and any texts or emails showing late nights and weekends. Then compare your real job, and your salary, to the tests above. If your work doesn't match the exemption your employer claims, those extra hours may be overtime you're owed.

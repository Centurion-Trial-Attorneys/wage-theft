**The short version:** Sometimes, but only if the rounding is neutral and doesn't leave workers underpaid over time. Your employer can never round your meal break punches. If your system automatically deducts 30 minutes for lunch and you worked through it, you're owed pay for that time and possibly a meal break premium.
</div>

Rounding and auto-deducts are quiet ways to lose pay. Nobody tells you to work for free. The time clock just keeps a few minutes every day, and over a year those minutes add up to real money.

## What is time rounding?

Rounding means your employer's payroll system changes your actual punch times to a set increment, like the nearest 5, 6 or 15 minutes. A common quarter-hour rule rounds 7 minutes or less down and 8 minutes or more up.

Here's how that plays out. Under that rule, clock in at 7:53 and you're paid from 8:00, so you lose 7 minutes. Clock in at 8:07 and you're also paid from 8:00, so you gain 7. On paper, it evens out. In practice, it often doesn't.

## Is rounding legal in California?

California courts have allowed it, with limits. In *See's Candy Shops, Inc. v. Superior Court* (2012), a Court of Appeal held that an employer can use a rounding policy if it's fair and neutral on its face and is used in a way that won't, over time, result in failing to pay employees for all the time they worked. That test comes from a federal regulation (29 C.F.R. § 785.48(b)). Another Court of Appeal upheld a quarter-hour rounding policy under the same test in *AHMC Healthcare, Inc. v. Superior Court* (2018).

The California Supreme Court has never approved that test. It may be about to decide it.

## What is the Home Depot rounding case?

In *Camp v. Home Depot U.S.A., Inc.* (2022), a worker's electronic punch records showed he lost a net 470 minutes, about 7.8 hours, to Home Depot's quarter-hour rounding. The Court of Appeal held that when an employer can capture and has captured the exact time an employee worked, it must pay the employee for all that time. Home Depot couldn't use its rounding policy to get the worker's claim thrown out.

The California Supreme Court agreed to review the case in 2023. As of late October 2026, it's still on the Court's list of pending cases. The question is whether California employers may round at all. When the Court rules, the answer will matter to almost every hourly worker in the state.

## When does rounding cheat workers?

Even under the older test, rounding has to be neutral in practice as well as on paper. Watch for these patterns:

- **You can't clock in early, but you can clock out late.** If the system blocks early punches but lets late punches round down, the rounding only runs one way.
- **You're told to be ready before the clock starts.** If you have to be on the floor, logged in, or in uniform at 8:00 sharp, the minutes you punch in before 8:00 are work. Rounding them away is the same problem as [off-the-clock work](/articles/off-the-clock-work-california/).
- **Your punch times don't match your pay stub.** If the hours paid are almost always a little lower than the punches, the rounding isn't neutral for you.

Small losses grow. Losing an average of 4 minutes a shift, 5 shifts a week, for 50 weeks comes to 1,000 minutes, almost 17 hours of unpaid work in a year.

## Can my employer round my meal break punches?

No. The California Supreme Court held that employers cannot round time punches for meal periods (*Donohue v. AMN Services, LLC* (2021)). Meal period rules are strict about timing, and rounding can hide a short or late lunch. Say you clock out for lunch at 12:04 and back in at 12:31. That's a 27-minute break, which is short. Rounding both punches to the quarter hour would make it look like a full 30 minutes, 12:00 to 12:30.

The Court also held that when time records show missed, short or late meal periods, the law presumes a violation. Your employer then has to show it actually provided a proper break.

## Is an automatic meal deduction legal?

Many timekeeping systems subtract 30 minutes from every shift over a certain length, whether you took lunch or not. California's wage orders require employers to keep records showing when each work period begins and ends, and to record meal periods (see, for example, Wage Order 4, § 7(A)(3)). An auto-deduct records the lunch the system assumed. It doesn't show whether you actually got one.

If you worked through that deducted half hour, you weren't paid for it. That time counts toward your daily and weekly hours, and most hourly workers earn time and a half after 8 hours in a day or 40 in a week (Lab. Code § 510). You may also be owed an extra hour of pay as a meal break premium. Our guide to [meal and rest breaks](/articles/meal-and-rest-breaks-california/) explains how those premiums work.

Auto-deducts also make your records look perfect. The *Donohue* presumption depends on what the time records show, and a system that stamps a clean 30-minute lunch on every shift hides the missed breaks. That's why your own notes matter.

## Is this happening to my coworkers too?

Almost certainly. Rounding rules and auto-deducts are set in the payroll system, so they apply to everyone on the same clock. A few minutes per worker per shift across a whole company can add up to a large amount, which is the kind of problem a [wage class action](/class-actions/) is built for. A worker can also seek penalties on behalf of coworkers under [PAGA](/articles/paga-explained/).

## How far back can I go?

Most claims for unpaid wages must be filed within three years (Code Civ. Proc. § 338(a)). Every month you wait, your oldest month of lost minutes can fall outside that window.

## What to do right now

- **Screenshot your raw punches.** Many timekeeping apps show your exact in and out times. Save them before they change or disappear.
- **Compare punches to your pay stub.** Add up the hours you punched and the hours you were paid. Note any gap.
- **Log your lunches.** Each day, write down when your meal break actually started and ended, or that you didn't get one.
- **Note the rules around the clock,** like early punch lockouts or instructions to be ready before your shift starts.
- **Don't take company documents** you aren't allowed to have. Your own notes and screenshots are enough to start.

A few minutes a day is still your money. If your punches say one thing and your paycheck says another, get it looked at.

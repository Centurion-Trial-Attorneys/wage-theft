<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
**The short version:** In California, anyone paid to work is treated as an employee unless the company proves all three parts of the "ABC test" (Lab. Code § 2775). A 1099, a "contractor agreement," or a job title doesn't decide it. If you're misclassified, you can be owed overtime, break premiums, minimum wage, and reimbursement for work expenses you paid yourself.
</div>

Companies love the word "contractor." It lets them skip overtime, breaks, payroll taxes, and workers' comp. California law looks at how you actually work.

## What is California's ABC test?

A person who provides labor or services for pay is an employee "rather than an independent contractor unless the hiring entity demonstrates" that all three of these conditions are met (Lab. Code § 2775(b)(1)):

- **A. You're free from the company's control and direction** in how you do the work, both under the contract and in fact.
- **B. Your work is outside the usual course of the company's business.**
- **C. You're customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, or business** of the same kind as the work you do for them.

The company has to prove all three. If it fails even one, you're an employee.

The test comes from the California Supreme Court's decision in *Dynamex Operations West, Inc. v. Superior Court* (2018) 4 Cal.5th 903. The Legislature later wrote it into the Labor Code.

## Does my 1099 or contractor agreement decide it?

No. The Labor Commissioner's Office says that being called an independent contractor, signing an agreement saying so, or getting a 1099 instead of a W-2 does not determine your status. Your employer can't turn you into a contractor with paperwork.

## What are the warning signs I'm misclassified?

Look at how the job works day to day. Common red flags:

- **The company sets your schedule** or tells you when to log in and when to show up.
- **You use their tools, uniform, vehicle markings, software, or scripts.**
- **You do the company's core work.** A delivery company's drivers or a cleaning company's cleaners are doing exactly what the business sells. That's prong B.
- **You can't work for competitors,** or you don't realistically have other clients.
- **You're paid by the hour or by the shift** instead of negotiating a price for a project.
- **You're supervised, trained, rated, or disciplined** like the employees around you.

No single sign settles it, but together they tell a story.

## What can a misclassified worker be owed?

If you're an employee, the wage laws apply to the whole time you were misclassified. That can include:

- **Overtime.** Most hourly employees earn time and a half after 8 hours in a day or 40 in a week, and double time after 12 hours in a day (Lab. Code § 510). See [unpaid overtime in California](/articles/unpaid-overtime-california/).
- **Meal and rest break premiums.** One extra hour of pay for each workday a required meal or rest period wasn't provided (Lab. Code § 226.7). See [meal and rest breaks in California](/articles/meal-and-rest-breaks-california/).
- **Minimum wage** for every hour worked, including hours you were never paid for (Lab. Code § 1194).
- **Business expenses.** Your employer must reimburse "all necessary expenditures or losses" you incur doing the job (Lab. Code § 2802(a)). Think mileage, gas, phone plans, tools, and supplies the company made you buy.
- **Final pay penalties** if you've left the job and your employer willfully failed to pay everything you earned on time (Lab. Code § 203). See [final paychecks and waiting time penalties](/articles/final-paycheck-waiting-time-penalties/).

Misclassification can also stick you with the employer's share of payroll taxes and shut you out of unemployment insurance and workers' comp.

Most unpaid wage claims must be filed within three years (Code Civ. Proc. § 338), and every pay period that falls outside that window is money you can't get back.

## Are there penalties for willful misclassification?

Yes. It's illegal to willfully misclassify a worker as an independent contractor, meaning voluntarily and knowingly (Lab. Code § 226.8). If the Labor and Workforce Development Agency or a court finds a violation, the employer faces a civil penalty of $5,000 to $15,000 for each violation. If the employer has a pattern or practice of doing it, the range rises to $10,000 to $25,000 per violation (Lab. Code § 226.8(b), (c)).

How that money reaches workers depends on who brings the case. When the Labor Commissioner or a public prosecutor enforces the law, they can recover these penalties as damages paid to the worker. A worker can instead pursue the penalty through a PAGA claim, but not both for the same violation (Lab. Code § 226.8(g)(2)). Under PAGA, penalties are split between the state and the affected workers. Learn more in [PAGA explained](/articles/paga-explained/).

## Are some jobs exempt from the ABC test?

Yes. The Labor Code lists exemptions starting at Lab. Code § 2776, including certain business-to-business arrangements that meet a list of conditions. Some licensed professions, such as lawyers, accountants, and physicians, are also exempt (Lab. Code § 2783). For exempt situations, the older *Borello* test decides your status instead (*S. G. Borello & Sons, Inc. v. Department of Industrial Relations* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 341). An exemption doesn't automatically make you a contractor. It changes which test applies.

App-based rideshare and delivery drivers are a special category. Under Proposition 22, they can be treated as independent contractors when the company meets certain conditions, such as not setting their schedules or barring them from other apps (Bus. & Prof. Code § 7451).

## Does misclassification affect my coworkers too?

Usually. Companies rarely misclassify one person by accident. A whole category of workers (every driver, every installer, every technician) signs the same agreement and gets paid the same way. That makes misclassification a common basis for a [wage class action](/class-actions/), where one or more workers bring the case on behalf of everyone treated the same way. You don't need to recruit coworkers to start.

## What should I do if I think I'm misclassified?

- **Keep your 1099s, contracts, and pay records,** plus texts and emails about schedules and assignments.
- **Track your expenses:** mileage, gas, phone bills, tools, and uniforms.
- **Write down your real hours,** including time spent waiting, driving between jobs, and doing paperwork.
- **Don't wait.** The three-year clock keeps running on older pay periods.

The takeaway: the label on your paperwork doesn't settle anything. If you work like an employee, California law likely treats you as one, and you may be owed back pay for every week they called you something else.

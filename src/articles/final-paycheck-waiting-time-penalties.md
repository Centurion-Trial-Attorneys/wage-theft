<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
**The short version:** If you're fired or laid off in California, your final paycheck is due immediately, on your last day. If you quit, it's due on your last day when you gave at least 72 hours' notice, or within 72 hours when you didn't. If your employer willfully pays late, it owes you a penalty of one day's wages for each day it's late, up to 30 days.
</div>

"We'll mail it with the next payroll." California law doesn't care about your employer's payroll calendar. The deadlines for your last paycheck are short, and missing them costs money.

## When is my final paycheck due if I'm fired or laid off?

Immediately. When an employer discharges you, all wages earned and unpaid at that moment are due right then (Lab. Code § 201), not on the next regular payday. The Labor Commissioner's Office applies the same rule to layoffs.

## What if I quit?

It depends on how much notice you gave (Lab. Code § 202):

- **You gave at least 72 hours' notice:** your final pay is due on your last day.
- **You quit without 72 hours' notice:** your final pay is due within 72 hours.

If you quit without notice, you can ask the employer to mail your check to an address you give them. The date it's mailed counts as the payment date (Lab. Code § 202).

## Are there exceptions for certain industries?

A few narrow ones. Seasonal workers laid off from curing, canning, or drying perishable fruit, fish, or vegetables can be paid within a reasonable time, not to exceed 72 hours (Lab. Code § 201). Laid-off oil drilling workers can be paid within 24 hours, not counting weekends and holidays (Lab. Code § 201.7). Some motion picture production workers can be paid by the next regular payday (Lab. Code § 201.5).

## Does my final paycheck include vacation or PTO?

Yes. Earned, unused vacation is wages, and your employer has to pay it out at your final rate of pay when you leave (Lab. Code § 227.3). A policy can't make you forfeit vested vacation when you leave.

The final check should also include all hours worked through your last day, plus any overtime you earned (see [our overtime guide](/articles/unpaid-overtime-california/)). The Labor Commissioner's Office says earned commissions are due on the same timeline as the rest of your final pay.

## What is a waiting time penalty?

It's the price an employer pays for holding your money. If an employer willfully fails to pay final wages on time, your wages keep running as a penalty at the same rate, from the day they were due until they're paid or you file an action, for up to 30 days (Lab. Code § 203).

Here's how that works in real numbers. Say you earned $25 an hour and worked 8-hour days. Your daily rate is $200.

- Your employer pays your final check 12 days late. Penalty: 12 × $200 = **$2,400**.
- Your employer never pays. The penalty stops growing at 30 days: 30 × $200 = **$6,000**, on top of the wages you're still owed.

The Labor Commissioner's Office counts those days as calendar days, so weekends count. Being one day late is enough to start the clock.

## What does "willful" mean?

It doesn't require bad intent or a plan to cheat you. Under the state regulation, a failure to pay is willful when the employer intentionally fails to pay wages when they're due (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 8, § 13520). Choosing to hold your check until the next payroll run can still qualify.

The main defense is a "good faith dispute." That exists only when the employer has a real legal or factual defense that, if it won, would mean you're owed nothing. A defense with no evidence behind it, an unreasonable one, or one raised in bad faith doesn't count (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 8, § 13520). You also lose the penalty for any period when you hid from or refused full payment that was offered to you (Lab. Code § 203).

## How long do I have to claim waiting time penalties?

You can sue for waiting time penalties any time before the deadline runs on a lawsuit for the underlying wages (Lab. Code § 203(b)). The California Supreme Court held that this gives workers a single three-year deadline for these penalties, even when the employer eventually paid the wages late (*Pineda v. Bank of America, N.A.* (2010) 50 Cal.4th 1389).

## Does this affect my coworkers too?

Often, yes. Late final pay is usually a policy problem. A company that pays everyone on the next payday after they leave, or wipes out unused vacation for every departing worker, is doing it to the whole crew. If dozens of people were laid off on the same day and nobody got paid that day, every one of them may have a waiting time claim.

When the same practice hits a group of workers, it can be handled as a [wage class action](/class-actions/). It may also support penalties under the Private Attorneys General Act (see [our PAGA guide](/articles/paga-explained/)).

## How do I ask for my final pay?

Put it in writing. Send a short email or letter to HR or your manager that says:

1. The date your employment ended and whether you were let go or quit (and when you gave notice).
2. That you have not received your final wages, or what's missing (hours, overtime, vacation, commissions).
3. Where to send payment.

Keep a copy, and write down the date and amount of any payment that arrives. Those dates are how the penalty gets counted. It's illegal for your employer to retaliate against you for complaining about unpaid wages (Lab. Code § 98.6).

## Should I file a wage claim or a lawsuit?

You can file a wage claim with the Labor Commissioner's Office, a state agency process you can start without a lawyer, or you can file a lawsuit in court. A lawsuit is the usual route when other wage problems are mixed in (like [off-the-clock work](/articles/off-the-clock-work-california/) or missed breaks) or when coworkers were affected the same way.

## The takeaway

Write down the date you were fired, laid off, or quit, and the date your final pay actually showed up. Check that check for every hour, every overtime hour, and every vacation day. If it was late or short, you may be owed up to 30 days of extra wages, and so might everyone who left with you.

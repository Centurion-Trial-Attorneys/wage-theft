<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
**The short version:** Most hourly workers in California get a 30-minute meal break before the end of their fifth hour of work and a paid 10-minute rest break for every four hours worked or major fraction thereof. Both breaks have to be free of work and free of your employer's control. For each workday your employer doesn't provide a required meal or rest break, it owes you one extra hour of pay at your regular rate (Lab. Code § 226.7).
</div>

Breaks are easy to take from workers because each one looks small. Over a few years, a whole crew can be owed real money.

## When do I get a meal break in California?

If you work more than 5 hours in a day, your employer has to give you a meal period of at least 30 minutes (Lab. Code § 512). The California Supreme Court has held that it must start no later than the end of your fifth hour of work (*Brinker Restaurant Corp. v. Superior Court* (2012)). A lunch that starts at hour five and a half is late.

Work more than 10 hours and you get a second 30-minute meal period, starting no later than the end of your tenth hour (Lab. Code § 512; *Brinker*).

## Can I waive my meal break?

Only within tight limits, and only by mutual agreement (Lab. Code § 512):

- **First meal break:** can be skipped only if your whole workday is 6 hours or less.
- **Second meal break:** can be skipped only if your workday is 12 hours or less and you didn't waive the first one.

## When do I get rest breaks?

Rest breaks come from the wage order for your industry. The standard rule is 10 minutes of net rest time for every 4 hours worked "or major fraction thereof," with no rest break required if your total workday is under 3½ hours (for example, Wage Order 4, Cal. Code Regs., tit. 8, § 11040, subd. 12(A)). In practice (*Brinker*):

- **3½ to 6 hours:** one 10-minute break
- **More than 6 and up to 10 hours:** two
- **More than 10 and up to 14 hours:** three

Rest breaks are paid. They count as hours worked, and your employer can't dock your pay for them (Lab. Code § 226.7(d)).

## What counts as a real break?

For meal periods, your employer has to relieve you of all duty, give up control over what you do, and give you a reasonable chance to take an uninterrupted 30 minutes without pressuring or discouraging you (*Brinker*). It doesn't have to force you to stop working, but it can't push you to skip lunch or schedule you so tightly that a break is impossible.

If you aren't relieved of all duty, the meal period is "on duty" and counts as time worked. That's allowed only when the nature of the work prevents relief from all duty and you agreed in writing, and the agreement has to say you can revoke it in writing at any time (Wage Order 4, § 11(A)).

Rest breaks have to be off duty too. In *Augustus v. ABM Security Services, Inc.* (2016), the Supreme Court held that employers must relieve workers of all duties and give up control during rest periods, and that on-call rest periods don't satisfy the law. Keeping your radio on and staying ready to jump back in probably isn't a rest break.

## What am I owed if I miss a break?

For each workday your employer doesn't provide a required meal period, it owes you one additional hour of pay. The same goes for each workday it doesn't provide a required rest period (Lab. Code § 226.7(c)). The wage orders set out the meal premium and the rest premium separately (Wage Order 4, §§ 11(B), 12(B)), so a day with a missed lunch and a missed rest break can mean two extra hours.

That extra hour is paid at your "regular rate of compensation." The Supreme Court held this means the same regular rate used for overtime, so it includes nondiscretionary pay like earned bonuses on top of your hourly wage (*Ferra v. Loews Hollywood Hotel, LLC* (2021)). The ruling applies retroactively. Premiums paid at base rate only may be short too.

## What if my employer auto-deducts my lunch?

Many timekeeping systems knock 30 minutes off every shift whether you took lunch or not. If you worked through it, you weren't paid. You may also be owed the meal premium, and the unpaid time can push you into overtime. See [unpaid overtime in California](/articles/unpaid-overtime-california/) and [off-the-clock work](/articles/off-the-clock-work-california/).

Employers also can't round meal period punches. When time records show missed, short, or late meal periods, the law presumes a violation unless the employer proves otherwise (*Donohue v. AMN Services, LLC* (2021)). An auto-deduct system that records a perfect lunch every day hides that, which is why your notes matter.

## How far back can I recover missed break pay?

The Supreme Court treats break premiums as wages (*Murphy v. Kenneth Cole Productions, Inc.* (2007)), so most claims can reach back three years (Code Civ. Proc. § 338). Some can reach back four years under California's Unfair Competition Law (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17208). PAGA penalty claims have a shorter deadline, generally one year. Every month you wait, your oldest month can fall outside the window.

## Does this affect my coworkers too?

Usually. Break violations tend to come from a schedule, staffing level, or timekeeping setting that hits the whole crew. A policy that skips breaks across a workforce can support a [class action](/class-actions/) or a [PAGA claim](/articles/paga-explained/). You don't need to recruit coworkers to start.

## Do these rules apply to every job?

No. They cover most non-exempt (usually hourly) employees, and some wage orders differ. Certain construction, commercial driving, security, utility, and motion picture workers under qualifying union contracts follow different meal period rules (Lab. Code § 512(c)-(f)). Employees exempt from the break rules aren't covered by the premium (Lab. Code § 226.7(e)). If you're paid as an independent contractor, check whether you were [misclassified](/articles/misclassified-independent-contractor-california/). Misclassified workers can be owed break premiums.

## What to do now

Start a simple log today: when you clocked in, when your breaks started and ended, and what interrupted them. Keep your pay stubs and look for premium pay. Save messages telling you to skip or work through breaks. If missed breaks are routine at your job, they're probably routine for the people next to you, and the clock on the oldest ones is already running.
